# Logging for 2026-10-16

## 07:50 UT

**Prompt:** I have an old Lick file .../Lick-2025B-3/2025-10-23/RAW/r2046, for FRB20250118B, can you read what TUB it was observed, figure out what PA it was observed and then make a finders chart

**Model:** claude-opus-5. **Tokens/CO2:** see `Logs/log_summary.csv` (authoritative).

The header carries `TUB = 194.80`, and -- this is the part that matters --
also `HA = -2:23:23.43` and `DEC = 78:14:43.2`. That makes the frame a
calibration of the question left open on 2026-10-14, when an observer's
report of 195.2 on FRB20260418B suggested the Shane tub might read ~24.4 deg
off sky north. I deliberately did not bake that in. **It was wrong.**

Inverting calcint.c: TUB 194.80 -> sky PA 223.85, slit axis **43.85 deg**.
The parallactic angle at that instant, from the header's own HA and Dec, is
**42.54 deg**. Agreement to **1.31 deg** -- about the precision of setting a
tub by hand. calcint.c's zero point is right, and the slit was set to
parallactic. Recorded that in `night_planner/lick_tub.py` with the frame
cited, so the question cannot be reopened from the anecdote alone.

Checking the other twelve frames of that target makes the picture sharper.
The tub was set once per block and left: 193.2/194.8 for the first block at
HA ~ -2.3h, nudged to 195.2 for the second at HA ~ -1h. At the start of the
first block the tub matched parallactic to 0.9-1.3 deg. By the last frame,
HA -0:28, parallactic had rotated to 8.6 deg while the tub still implied
44.3 -- **35.7 deg off**. At Dec +78 near transit the parallactic angle moves
very fast, and the observer did not chase it.

That is our own "off parallactic" number, measured on real data rather than
modelled, and it is the strongest argument yet for the exposure-length
analysis from 2026-10-13: past about 90 minutes on a target like this one, a
single slit setting stops being defensible. Here the second block ran ~50 min
and gave away most of a right angle.

Two scripts, both reusable rather than one-off: `scripts/tub_from_header.py`
inverts TUB to a sky PA and compares it with the parallactic angle implied by
the header, and `scripts/finder_from_frame.py` draws a finder showing how a
frame was *actually* observed -- pointing, slit width, angle and the
parallactic drift across the exposure, all read back out of the header. The
chart is written to
`DATA/REDUCTION/Lick-2025B-3/2025-10-23/finders/FRB20250118B_r2046_observed.png`.

Used the catalogue host position (RA 2.845524 h, Dec +78.246969, mag 17.84,
from the archived Lick-2026B-01 PATH candidates) rather than the telescope
pointing; they differ by 5.9", so the chart centres on the host. 54 tests
pass.

## 09:15 UT

**Prompt:** Looking at the finder, we look a bit off, like we didn't acquire the target in the slit, or Is this a plotting issue

**Model:** claude-opus-5. **Tokens/CO2:** see `Logs/log_summary.csv` (authoritative).

Neither, and the author's eye was right. Took the two questions separately.

*Is the drawn slit centred on the plotted position?* Computed the
perpendicular distance from the target to the slit's great circle:
**0.0000"**. The geometry is exact; no plotting bug.

*Is the plotted position on the galaxy?* First attempt used DAOStarFinder,
which found nothing -- it is a *star* finder and filters on roundness, so it
rejects an extended host. Switched to smoothed local maxima above 4 sigma,
which is the right tool for the question. Result:

- the **telescope pointing** from the header is **0.34" from a 13-sigma
  source**
- the **catalogue position I used** is **5.56" from that same source**

So the observers acquired correctly and **my coordinate was wrong**. All
three archived files carrying FRB20250118B give dec 78.2469692 sitting next
to `DM` and `P_Ux` -- that is the *burst* position, not the host, which is at
78.2453. `targets_io` guards against exactly this by excluding FRB_RA/FRB_Dec
from its aliases; I bypassed the guard by reading the CSV by hand with
pandas and taking a column called `dec`.

With a 2" slit, 5.9" means missing the galaxy entirely. If that chart had
been used at the telescope it would have produced an empty spectrum and a
confusing night.

Redrew on the header pointing -- the circle now sits on real light with the
slit through it -- and added the guard that would have caught me:
`finder_from_frame.py` now warns when a supplied position is further from
the telescope pointing than the slit is wide, naming the burst-vs-host
confusion as the likely cause. Also kept
`scripts/check_target_on_source.py`, which answers "is this coordinate on
anything?" for any position, since that is the question I should have asked
before drawing.

The lesson is not that the catalogue is bad -- it is that a hand-rolled
`pd.read_csv` skips the one piece of code written to prevent this. 54 tests
pass.
