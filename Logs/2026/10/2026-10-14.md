## 05:30 UT

**Prompt:** I think we are doing it wrong, read the codes in /home/lordrick/Projects/night-planner/helpful_scripts

**Model:** claude-opus-5. **Tokens/CO2:** see `Logs/log_summary.csv` (authoritative).

`helpful_scripts/` holds `pa.c` and `calcint.c` -- Jim Burrous's 1990 Lick
Observatory utility (following Filippenko 1982 PASP) and its Cassegrain tub
interpolation table. These are the tools Lick observers actually use, so the
right move was to check our work against them rather than argue with them.

Ported both to Python (`scripts/lick_cass_tub.py`) and ran the comparison.
Three findings, and the one that matters is not the one I expected.

**Our parallactic angle is correct.** Across 441 HA/Dec points from Dec -20 to
+85 and HA -5 to +5, `night_planner.parallactic` agrees with `pa.c` to 0.000000
deg -- exactly, not approximately. Interesting difference in method: pa.c uses
`asin(sin HA cos lat / cos alt)`, which cannot separate the two solutions, so
it works with |HA| and then patches the quadrant by testing HA against
`acos(tan lat / tan dec)` for targets north of the latitude. We use the
four-quadrant `atan2(sin HA, tan lat cos dec - sin dec cos HA)`, which needs
no special case. Same answer, fewer branches. Refactored
`parallactic_angle()` into `parallactic_angle_from_ha()` + `hour_angle()` so
the comparison could be done at the formula level.

**The midpoint-vs-average question is a non-issue tonight.** pa.c evaluates
the angle once at the midpoint of the exposure; we take a tan(z)-weighted
average over the block. For tonight's eight targets the two differ by 0.10 to
0.64 deg. Our version is the better one for a long exposure at low altitude,
and it costs nothing to keep -- but it is worth knowing that a Lick observer
checking a plan against `pa.c` will see the midpoint number.

**What we were actually doing wrong: the tub.** `calcint.c` exists because the
Shane does not take a sky PA. The instrument hangs in a Cassegrain tub read
out in Nixie units, and the mapping is `readout = 0.87 x PA + 0.05`. PA 108.92
is tub **94.81**, not 108.92. Our starlist has been emitting `PA=108.92` to an
observer who needs 94.81 -- dial the PA and the slit is 16 deg off. This is
precisely the class of instrument quirk the planner exists to remember, and we
had the sky angle right and the operational number missing.

Added `night_planner/lick_tub.py` (table verbatim from calcint.c, interpolated
the same way), a `rotator: 'cass_tub'` flag on the kast site entry, the tub on
every Lick finder chart caption and sidecar, and `#TUB = 94.81 or 251.41` on
each Lick starlist target row with a header line saying the sky PA is not the
tub. Both settings are always quoted, 180 deg apart, because which one is
reachable depends on where the tub already sits -- the same "(or ...)" pa.c
prints.

One bug caught by its own test: I first normalised the angle with `% 360`,
which folds 380 onto 20 and reads 17.45 instead of 330.64. The table runs
20..380 precisely so its last 20 degrees repeat the first 20; only angles
outside that range may be wrapped. Five new tests in `tests/test_lick_tub.py`,
including one asserting the conversion is *not* the identity. 29 tests pass.

Three axes, because each breaks something different: **season** (one FFFF-PZ

hour-angle against the mount limits. A Dec -30 target culminates at alt 22.7
deg from Lick (airmass 2.6) and still schedules happily, where the Keck path
would have dropped it at AM 2.0. That asymmetry is deliberate as far as the
mount is concerned, but it means a southern filler can eat an hour for a

## 14:30 UT

**Prompts:** Can you read .../ryan_scheduler/AstroScheduler and compare to ours / Do they calculate slew time? / what about exposure times? how do they do it?

**Model:** claude-opus-5. **Tokens/CO2:** see `Logs/log_summary.csv` (authoritative).

Read UCSC AstroScheduler (2161-line scheduler.py, 6039-line app.js) against
ours. The short version: theirs is the better *scheduler*, ours the better
*night-plan generator*, with almost no overlap in the hard parts. Theirs has a
branch-and-bound solver over 1-minute chunks with airmass cost and suffix
pruning, per-minute feasibility checks, a real-time "reschedule from now"
mode, Playwright tests and CI. Ours has everything downstream of the
timeline -- finders, offset stars, starlists, parallactic angles, the Cass
tub, FFFF-PZ, Excel, rejection accounting -- none of which exists in theirs
(grep for finder/starlist/parallactic/slit returns nothing).

Their per-minute `is_visible()` is architecturally better than our analytic
window arithmetic, and would have made this morning's HA-wrap bug impossible.
Worth stealing.

Two follow-up questions turned up the same failure mode twice: **code that is
computed and then discarded.**

*Slew time.* `get_slew_time()` has real per-telescope logic (7 min base, 15
crossing the equator at Keck) and is called in exactly one place, where the
result goes into a comment string. The block duration is a hardcoded
`7 + ceil(t_seq/60)` in all five return paths of
`get_target_exposure_details()`. So the solver reserves a flat 7 min whatever
the slew, and the Keck penalty is printed to the observer and ignored by the
scheduler. `SLEW_ACQ_OVERHEAD_MIN = 7.0` is defined and never referenced;
app.js defines its own at 5.0, also never referenced, disagreeing with Python.

*Exposure times.* Same pattern, and this one corrects what I told the author
an hour earlier. I had listed "moon and extinction in the exposure model" as a
strength of theirs. It is dead code: `Target.calculate_exposure_time()` --
the only physics in the project, with magnitude scaling, extinction and a
moon-brightness term -- is called by nothing but the tests.
`get_target_exposure_details()` takes `moon` and `extinction` as parameters
and uses neither. Everything real comes from hardcoded lookups on magnitude
rounded to 0.5. Checking call sites before crediting a feature is the lesson;
the function existed and looked load-bearing.

Also found their LRIS and KCWI tables are byte-identical (verified by eval'ing
both and comparing), which cannot be right for an IFU.

Wrote `scripts/compare_exposure_models.py` rather than eyeball it. Their Kast
blocks run consistently shorter than ours, 1.07x to 1.88x, with the biggest
gap at the bright end: 19 min for a mag 15 host against our 35. Their table
clamps at mag 20, so a mag 23 FRB host gets the same 82-minute block as a mag
20 one; ours is a 4-tier step function calibrated against nine executed night
plans. Their 0.5-mag resolution is genuinely finer than our four tiers -- that
part is better than ours. Their LRIS table clamps at mag 24 and tops out at
2400 s, where our real ETC needs 3 x 7200 s for mag 22.6 and still only
reaches S/N 5.7.

Open question for the author: is 19 minutes really enough for a mag 15 host at
Kast? If their bright-end numbers are right, our plans are leaving time on the
table every night.
